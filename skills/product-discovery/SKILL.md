# Product Discovery

Turn an idea or reported pain into a decision grounded in evidence. Discovery may recommend building, testing, narrowing, or stopping. It does not exist to justify a solution already chosen.

## Frame the decision

State the decision this work must inform, the affected users and context, the desired user or business outcome, and the cost of being wrong. Separate four kinds of input:

- confirmed facts with a source;
- observed behavior or recurring evidence;
- interpretations and hypotheses;
- unknowns that could change the decision.

Translate solution-shaped requests into a problem hypothesis without discarding the request. “Add bulk actions” may point to a need to complete repetitive work with fewer errors; it does not prove that bulk actions are the best answer.

## Gather the strongest available evidence

Inspect supplied research, support cases, usage data, workflow recordings, prior attempts, and current workarounds. Prefer observed past behavior over stated future enthusiasm. Record who or what each signal represents; one loud report is not automatically a broad pattern.

Do not invent interviews, quotes, metrics, market facts, or user confidence. If evidence is thin, return a discovery plan rather than a confident verdict. External outreach, analytics queries, or changes to customer-facing systems require their own authorization.

## Map the opportunity

Connect the evidence in this order:

1. Desired outcome: the change that matters, stated observably where possible.
2. Opportunity: a user need, friction, risk, or unmet job that could affect the outcome.
3. Candidate approaches: meaningfully different ways to address the opportunity.
4. Assumptions: what must be true for each approach to work.
5. Evidence test: the cheapest safe test that could disconfirm the load-bearing assumption.

Keep opportunities distinct from features. Generate alternatives only when the solution space is genuinely open; do not manufacture three cosmetic variants to satisfy a quota.

## Return a decision brief

Include:

- the decision and current recommendation;
- the problem and target context;
- evidence supporting and weakening the hypothesis;
- opportunities and candidate approaches;
- the most consequential assumptions and unknowns;
- the next evidence-gathering action, with a stopping condition;
- what should not be built or decided yet.

Calibrate the recommendation as `proceed`, `test first`, `reframe`, or `stop`. Explain what new evidence would change it. Do not turn discovery directly into implementation; a selected direction still needs an approved product specification or prototype boundary.

## Boundaries

- Do not use fake precision or multiply subjective scores into a scientific-looking verdict.
- Do not treat an existing workaround as proof that a particular solution will succeed.
- Do not equate user requests with underlying needs or business priority.
- Do not hide contradictory evidence or empty searches.
- Keep employer and customer facts in their authorized project systems, not in this portable skill.
